Councils buy works, services and goods all year, often in contract sizes a small firm can deliver. Knowing how to win council contracts comes down to three habits. Find the right opportunities early, meet the entry requirements without last-minute scrambling, and write a bid that answers the published scoring criteria. This guide covers how councils in England, Wales and Northern Ireland buy under the [Procurement Act 2023](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/54/contents), which has applied to new procurements since 24 February 2025, and what that means for your next bid.

## The short answer

To win a council contract, register your business on Find a Tender, the government's central digital platform. Track notices in your sector so you see work before the deadline is close. Bid only where you meet the conditions of participation. Then answer every question against the published award criteria and weightings, with evidence, a realistic price and specific social value commitments.

## How councils buy under the Procurement Act 2023

The Act and the Procurement Regulations 2024 set the rules for most public buyers in England, Wales and Northern Ireland, councils included. The estimated value of a contract, including VAT, decides how much of the Act applies.

### The thresholds that matter for councils

Councils count as sub-central government authorities. These thresholds apply to procurements that start on or after 1 January 2026 and run to 31 December 2027 ([Thresholds guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/procurement-act-2023-guidance-documents-define-phase/guidance-thresholds-html), [PPN 023: 2026 threshold amounts](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ppn-023-2026-threshold-amounts)).

| Contract type | Threshold including VAT |
| --- | --- |
| Goods or services bought by a council | £207,720 |
| Works contracts, such as construction | £5,193,000 |
| Light touch contracts (services listed in the regulations, including many health, social and education services) | £663,540 |

A contract at or above its threshold is a "public contract" and follows the full rules. Below it, the lighter rules in Part 6 of the Act apply. Because the works threshold is so much higher, a lot of [construction and infrastructure](/categories/construction-infrastructure) work runs under below-threshold rules even at seven-figure values.

### Above threshold: the tender notice and two procedures

A council starts a competitive tender for a public contract by publishing a tender notice on the central digital platform. The council then uses one of two procedures ([Competitive tendering procedures guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/procurement-act-2023-guidance-documents-define-phase/competitive-tendering-procedures-html)).

- The open procedure. Any supplier can submit a tender, and the council assesses all tenders in one stage.
- The competitive flexible procedure. The council designs its own stages. It might shortlist suppliers first, then add negotiation, presentations or a site visit, and the tender notice must explain the stages.

Minimum time limits are set in [section 54](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/54/section/54). The council must also allow for the contract's complexity, so the minimum is a floor rather than the norm.

Two duties work in your favour. Before publishing the tender notice, the council must consider splitting the contract into lots, and give reasons if lots would work but it doesn't use them ([section 18](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/54/section/18)). Throughout the procurement, it must have regard to the barriers small and medium-sized enterprises face and consider whether they can be removed or reduced ([section 12(4)](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/54/section/12)).

### Below threshold: lighter rules, and the ones that still apply

Many smaller local authority tenders in the UK sit below the thresholds. The council has more freedom here, but some rules still protect bidders ([Below-threshold contracts guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/procurement-act-2023-guidance-documents-define-phase/guidance-below-threshold-contracts-html)).

- If a council advertises a contract worth £30,000 or more including VAT, it must first publish a below-threshold tender notice on the central digital platform and give every supplier the same reasonable time to respond.
- It doesn't have to advertise. It can ask selected suppliers for quotes instead, but it must still publish a contract details notice for any award of £30,000 or more.
- It can't run a separate suitability stage, such as a pre-qualification questionnaire, before tenders come in, except for works contracts above the goods and services threshold.
- It must consider the barriers SMEs face, and the implied 30-day payment terms apply.

Councils and other best value authorities in England can now reserve below-threshold competitions to UK-based suppliers or to suppliers based in a defined local area. If a council does this, the advertisement must state what the local area is ([Reserving competitions for below-threshold contracts](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/reserving-competitions-for-below-threshold-contracts/guidance-on-reserving-competitions-for-below-threshold-contracts)). Procurement by schools is outside the below-threshold rules.

### Scotland and Wales

Scottish councils mostly buy under Scotland's own procurement law, not the 2023 Act, and advertise on Public Contracts Scotland. Welsh councils follow the Act with Welsh-specific provisions, and Sell2Wales is the Welsh national portal.

## Where council tenders are published

Selling to local government in the UK means watching several places at once. Our guide on [how to find public sector tenders](/guides/how-to-find-public-sector-tenders) covers every buyer type; for councils, these are the places to watch.

- Find a Tender, the central digital platform. Since 24 February 2025, above-threshold and below-threshold notices for new procurements are published here, except below-threshold notices in Scotland ([Central digital platform factsheet](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/procurement-act-2023-short-guides/central-digital-platform-factsheet-html)).
- Contracts Finder. It still holds lower-value notices for procurements that started under the old rules. Our guide to Find a Tender vs Contracts Finder explains the split.
- The council's own e-tendering system. The notice is published centrally, but documents, clarification questions and submission often run through the council's chosen portal. You'll usually need a separate account for each one.

Some of the best signals come before a tender exists. A contracting authority that expects to pay more than £100 million under contracts in the coming financial year must publish a pipeline notice covering planned contracts of £2 million or more. Preliminary market engagement notices tell you a council wants supplier input, and a planned procurement notice tells you a tender is coming and may shorten the time you get to respond. The notice types guide explains each one.

## Frameworks and dynamic markets

Councils often buy through frameworks and dynamic markets rather than one-off tenders. If you aren't on the right one, you can't bid for the work awarded through it.

A framework normally lasts no more than four years ([section 47](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/54/section/47)), and you usually get on it by winning a competitive tender. An open framework must reopen to new suppliers at least once in its first three years and then at least every five years, up to eight years in total ([section 49](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/54/section/49)). A dynamic market, which replaces the old dynamic purchasing system, must accept applications at any time while it runs, and the council can't cap the number of members ([section 36](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/54/section/36)). Our guide to frameworks and dynamic markets covers how to get on them.

## Pre-bid checklist for council tenders for small businesses

Get these ready before a deadline is running.

1. Register on Find a Tender. Create a GOV.UK One Login, add your organisation and complete the supplier information, including connected persons. For public contracts, you share it with the council through the platform before the tendering period ends.
2. Check your insurance. Find out which cover councils in your sector ask for, typically employer's liability, public liability and professional indemnity. For public contracts, a council can't require the insurance to be in place before award ([section 22](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/54/section/22)), but expect to confirm you will have it.
3. Prepare your accounts. A council can ask for audited accounts only from businesses that are legally required to have them audited. If you're exempt from audit, expect to be asked for other financial information instead.
4. List your accreditations. Note which certifications your sector expects, for example Cyber Essentials for many [technology](/categories/technology) contracts. Councils must accept equivalents to any specific qualification they name.
5. Build three relevant case studies with the client type, scope and a measurable outcome, and permission to use them. A council can't insist your experience comes from a contract with one particular authority.
6. Keep your policies current. Health and safety, equality, data protection and environmental policies come up most often, plus safeguarding for work with children or vulnerable adults.
7. Draft your social value offer. Work out what you can commit to locally and measure, such as apprenticeships, local hiring, local supply-chain spend or emissions reductions on the contract.
8. Check the exclusion grounds. You'll self-declare against the mandatory and discretionary grounds in Schedules 6 and 7, which mostly look back five years. If one applies, prepare evidence of the steps you've taken to stop it happening again ([Exclusions guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/procurement-act-2023-guidance-documents-procure-phase/guidance-exclusions-html)).
9. Decide bid or no-bid. Check every pass/fail requirement, whether the value fits your turnover and whether you can staff it. If the contract is too big, a consortium partner's or subcontractor's capacity can count towards the conditions of participation.

## How councils score bids

Under the Act, a council must award a competitively tendered public contract to the most advantageous tender. That means the tender that meets the requirements and scores best against the published award criteria, using the published assessment methodology and weightings ([Assessing competitive tenders guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/procurement-act-2023-guidance-documents-procure-phase/assessing-competitive-tenders-html)). Most advantageous doesn't mean cheapest. Price can be the only criterion, but the guidance says this is unlikely to suit most contracts.

What the rules tell you about scoring:

- The criteria must relate to the subject matter of the contract, be clear and measurable, and be proportionate.
- The council must publish the relative importance of the criteria, and say if failing a pass/fail question disqualifies you.
- Price can be assessed on whole-life cost rather than purchase price, and the guidance warns councils about relative price scoring, where every price score is based on the lowest bid.
- In England, a council procuring services at or above the threshold must consider, before it starts, how the contract might improve the economic, social and environmental well-being of its area ([Public Services (Social Value) Act 2012, section 1](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2012/3/section/1)). Councils must also have regard to the [National Procurement Policy Statement](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/national-procurement-policy-statement). Expect social value questions in many council tenders as a result.
- If your price looks abnormally low, the council must give you a chance to show you can deliver at that price before it rejects the tender.
- A council may disregard a tender that breaks a procedural rule in the documents, such as a late submission or an answer over the word limit.

## How to win council contracts once the tender is live

Knowing how to bid for council contracts well means working to the evaluator's marking scheme.

- Build a compliance matrix on day one. List every question, word limit, attachment, pass/fail test and deadline, including the clarification deadline.
- Read the scoring descriptors. If a top score requires "evidence of delivery on comparable contracts", give that evidence, with names of client types, volumes and dates.
- Answer the question asked, in the order asked. Evaluators mark each answer on its own.
- Write for this contract. Refer to the service, sites, users and risks in the specification. Copy pasted from another bid scores poorly.
- Make social value measurable. "We support the local community" can't be scored. "Two apprenticeships recruited from the council's area in year one, reported quarterly" can.
- Ask clarification questions early. Answers are usually shared with all bidders, so word them carefully, but don't guess at an ambiguous requirement.
- Price to deliver. Build the price from a realistic resource plan and be ready to explain any line that looks low.
- Submit a day early. E-tendering portals can be slow near a deadline, and a late tender can be disregarded.

## Why SMEs lose council tenders

Most failed bids from small firms come down to a few avoidable mistakes.

- A missed pass/fail requirement, such as an accreditation or a signed declaration.
- A late or incomplete submission, or answers over the word count.
- Answers that describe the company instead of how it would deliver this contract, with claims and no evidence.
- A vague social value offer, or a price too high to compete or too low to be credible.
- A poor fit for the firm's size, where a lot or a subcontracting role would have suited better.
- Finding the tender too late to write a good response.

## After the decision

For a public contract, the council must give each supplier whose tender it assessed an assessment summary explaining the scores. It then publishes a contract award notice, which starts a standstill period of at least eight working days before it can sign the contract. Read your assessment summary whether you won or lost, and feed it into your next bid.

Once you're delivering, the council must pay a valid, undisputed invoice within 30 days of receiving it ([section 68](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/54/section/68)). Equivalent terms are implied into subcontracts under public contracts, so the same applies if you deliver as a subcontractor.

## FAQ

### Do I need to register on Find a Tender to bid for council contracts?

For public contracts, yes. You must be registered on the central digital platform and share your core supplier information with the council before the tendering period ends. You only need to register when you're ready to bid.

### What is the threshold for council tenders in 2026?

From 1 January 2026, it is £207,720 including VAT for goods or services, £5,193,000 for works and £663,540 for light touch services. Below these values lighter rules apply, but a council advertising a contract of £30,000 or more must still publish a notice on the central digital platform.

### Can a small business win a council contract without public sector experience?

Yes. A council can't require you to have held a contract with one particular authority. Below threshold, it can't run a separate suitability stage before tenders, except for higher-value works. Use relevant private sector case studies, and consider subcontracting to gain public sector references.

### How long do councils give you to respond to a tender?

For an above-threshold open procedure with electronic submission and all documents published at the start, the minimum is 25 days. It can be 10 days in some cases, for example after a qualifying planned procurement notice. Below threshold, the time must be reasonable and the same for every supplier.

### Can councils give contracts to local businesses?

Below threshold, councils in England can reserve a competition to UK-based or local suppliers and must define the local area in the advertisement. The option doesn't extend to above-threshold contracts, where councils must treat suppliers the same unless a difference justifies otherwise.
