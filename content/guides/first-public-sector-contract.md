## Why the first contract is the hard one

Small firms new to public contracting run into the same walls. Buyers ask for case studies and references from similar work. If you have never held a public contract, that can feel like a closed door. In practice, buyers cannot insist that you have worked for one particular authority, and comparable private sector work is often accepted. What they will not score well is a bid with no relevant evidence at all.

The bid itself is unpaid work. A first tender can consume weeks of method statements, policies, pricing and social value answers. Firms that treat that as "experience" sometimes miss the deadline, or discover a pass/fail requirement too late. Incumbents and larger suppliers start with reusable answers, accreditations, and often a relationship with the buyer. That does not mean the process is always stitched up. It does mean your first bid should be one you can finish to a high standard, not a flagship contract that would double your turnover overnight.

The useful response is not to bid for everything, and not to give up. It is to get ready, pick a realistic route in, and be selective.

## Get your compliance pack ready before you bid

Do this once, then keep it current. A surprising number of first bids fail, or are abandoned, because a mandatory document is missing in the last few days.

**Cyber Essentials.** This government-backed scheme tests a basic set of technical controls. It is not required for every public contract, and buyers must not apply it as a blanket rule. For in-scope organisations — central government departments, their executive agencies and non-departmental public bodies, and NHS bodies — Cyber Essentials or Cyber Essentials Plus, or equivalent controls, are required for certain higher-risk contracts, including those that handle personal information or supply ICT systems that store or process data at OFFICIAL ([PPN 014](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ppn-014-cyber-essentials-scheme/ppn-014-cyber-essentials-scheme-html)). Evidence is needed before award, and the certificate must be renewed annually during the contract. Other public bodies, and many [technology](/categories/technology) tenders, ask for it too. Start early if you will need it, and remember that buyers must accept equivalents.

**Insurance.** Expect to be asked about employer's liability, public liability and professional indemnity, and sometimes cyber insurance. For a public contract, a buyer cannot require the insurance to be in place before award, but you will need to show you will have it ([section 22](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/54/section/22)). Know the limits your sector usually asks for, and get quotes before you bid, so you are not guessing in the pricing schedule.

**Policies and basics.** Typical requests include data protection, health and safety, equality, and modern slavery. You do not need a binder of generic templates. You need short, current documents that describe what your firm actually does, signed and dated, that you can attach without rewriting them at midnight.

**Financial standing and legal form.** Buyers often want to contract with a company rather than a sole trader, and they will test financial capacity. They cannot demand audited accounts from a firm that is not required to have them audited. They also cannot require you to have held a contract with one named authority.

If a tender's pass/fail list includes something you cannot meet in time, it is a no bid. That is a cheaper lesson than a late night spent on a submission that will be set aside.

## Evidence without a public sector track record

Quality questions are scored on evidence: what you did, for whom, what changed, and what you would do on this contract. A sales pitch, or a promise to "work it out once we start", scores poorly.

You can use private sector case studies if they are comparable. Be specific about what you delivered, the timescale and the result, and be honest about scale. Evaluators can tell when a much larger contract is being bid by a firm whose largest job was a fraction of that size. Write two or three tight examples now, with permission to name the client or with the client anonymised if you must, so you are not drafting them during a live bid.

If you have no comparable work at all, a large open tender is the wrong starting point. Use the routes below, or a smaller lot, until you have something you can point to.

## Routes in that do not start with a huge open tender

These are the paths firms actually use to get a first reference.

**Subcontracting or a consortium.** Many first public contracts are delivered as a named subcontractor, or as a delivery partner, to a supplier that already holds the contract or a framework place. You gain a reference and learn how the work is run, while staying closer to what you can deliver. Agree roles, margins and payment terms in writing, and keep your compliance pack ready.

**Lots and size.** Bid for a lot that matches your capacity, not the whole contract. A first job that would double your business is a risk for you and for the buyer, and evaluators can see it.

**Below-threshold work.** For procurements starting between 1 January 2026 and 31 December 2027, goods or services sit above threshold at £135,018 including VAT for a central government authority and £207,720 for other public buyers such as a council; works sit at £5,193,000 ([PPN 023](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ppn-023-2026-threshold-amounts/ppn-023-2026-threshold-amounts-html)). Below those values, a buyer may invite selected suppliers to quote. If it does advertise a contract worth £12,000 or more from a central government authority, or £30,000 or more from other buyers, it must publish a below-threshold tender notice and give every supplier the same reasonable time ([Below-threshold contracts guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/procurement-act-2023-guidance-documents-define-phase/guidance-below-threshold-contracts-html)). That lighter process is often a better first bid than a multi-year flagship contract. In England, some buyers can also reserve a below-threshold competition to UK-based or local suppliers.

**Open frameworks and dynamic markets.** A lot of later work is only offered to suppliers already on a list. A standard framework can stay closed for years, but an open framework must reopen on a set cycle, and a dynamic market must accept applications at any time. [How to get on a public sector framework](/guides/how-to-get-on-a-framework) explains the difference. A place is not a first contract on its own, but it is how you become eligible for work that never appears as an open tender.

**Early conversations, used properly.** If the first time a buyer hears of you is when the tender lands, you are often behind. Watch pipeline and preliminary market engagement notices, which can appear months earlier, and take part when you have something useful to say. The buyer must make sure no supplier gets an unfair advantage from that engagement, and must exclude a supplier whose advantage cannot be avoided ([section 16](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/54/section/16)). [How to find public sector tenders](/guides/how-to-find-public-sector-tenders) explains those early notices.

## Choose the first tender carefully

Once you are ready to bid, be stricter than you think you need to be. Work through the questions in the [bid or no bid guide](/guides/bid-no-bid-decision): can you meet every pass/fail requirement, how much of the score is price, is there an incumbent, do you have comparable evidence, does the value fit your size, and do you have time to do it properly?

For a first bid, add three more filters.

- Prefer quality-weighted work in a niche you know, over a heavily price-weighted competition you will not win on day rate.
- Give yourself enough time. Finding a notice a few days before it closes is a common reason first bids are weak or late. If a planned procurement notice has already appeared, the buyer may be able to cut the minimum tendering period to 10 days, so start preparing when you see the early notice, not when the tender notice drops.
- Read the [social value](/guides/social-value-in-tenders) question early. A generic paragraph will not score. A specific, measurable commitment linked to the contract can.

[How to bid for and win council contracts](/guides/how-to-win-council-contracts) walks through scoring, thresholds and a pre-bid checklist if your first target is a local authority. The same habits apply to central government and other public bodies: answer the question asked, provide the evidence asked for, and do not rely on creativity to make up for a missing requirement.

## What a first bid should teach you

Even a well-chosen first tender can lose. Ask for feedback, and read it against the published criteria rather than a story about the process being closed. If you lost on evidence, write a better case study. If you lost on price, be honest about whether that type of tender is worth your time. If you missed a mandatory document, fix the pack before you bid again. Cost the hours at your normal rate so the next attempt is a commercial choice, not a habit. If you won, keep the evidence, reporting and social value commitments tidy. The second contract is easier when the first one is documented.

## FAQs

### Can I win with no public sector experience?

Yes, if you can evidence comparable work and meet the mandatory requirements. A buyer cannot demand that you have already worked for one particular authority.

### Do I need Cyber Essentials before I bid?

Not for every tender, and it must not be applied as a blanket rule. In-scope central government and NHS bodies must require it, or an equivalent, for certain contracts that handle personal or official data, and many other buyers ask for it too. Where it is required, you need the evidence before award ([PPN 014](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ppn-014-cyber-essentials-scheme/ppn-014-cyber-essentials-scheme-html)).

### Is subcontracting a sensible first step?

Often, yes, if the commercial terms are clear and you can still deliver the work. It is a common way to gain a relevant reference.

### Should I start with a council or with central government?

Whichever you can evidence and finish. Council work is covered in the [council contracts guide](/guides/how-to-win-council-contracts). Use the value band and the pass/fail list, not the logo, to decide.

### How long will a first bid take?

Longer than you think if you are building the pack as you go. Get policies, insurance, accreditations and case studies ready first, then bid only when you have time to read the documents, ask questions, price the work and review the submission.
