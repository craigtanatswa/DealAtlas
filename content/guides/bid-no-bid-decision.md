## Why the bid no bid decision matters

Ask owners of small UK firms about public sector tendering and the same complaints come up again and again. They describe weeks lost to long questionnaires, method statements and evidence packs. They describe losing to the same large suppliers, and wondering whether the contract was ever really open. Some say their first tender was a learning exercise rather than a sensible use of time.

People who have sat on evaluation panels tell a more useful story. Most tenders are scored fairly against the published criteria. Small firms lose because they bid for work that never suited them, miss a pass/fail requirement, or write a sales pitch instead of answering the question asked. Large firms win often because they have bid teams, reusable evidence and a habit of walking away from tenders they can't win.

That last habit is the one to copy. Walking away is a decision, not a failure.

## Seven questions to ask before you bid

Work through these in order. The first two can end the conversation in minutes.

### 1. Can you meet every pass/fail requirement?

Read the tender notice and documents for anything marked pass/fail, mandatory or a condition of participation. Typical examples are accreditations, insurance levels, financial standing, specific policies and declarations. Under the Procurement Act 2023 the buyer must say whether failing a requirement means you are out ([Assessing competitive tenders guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/procurement-act-2023-guidance-documents-procure-phase/assessing-competitive-tenders-html)). If you fail one and can't fix it before the deadline, stop.

### 2. How much of the score is price?

The buyer must publish the relative importance of each award criterion, as percentages, a ranking or another clear description. Read it early. Experienced bidders say that when price carries most of the marks, the cheapest compliant bid usually wins, and a small firm with a higher day rate rarely beats it on quality alone.

Look at how price is scored too. Government guidance warns buyers about relative scoring, where your price score depends on the lowest price submitted. If you know you won't be the cheapest, a heavily price-weighted tender is usually a no bid.

### 3. Is there an incumbent, and do they have an edge?

Many contracts are re-tenders of existing work. Switching supplier can be expensive and risky for the buyer, so the incumbent often starts in front. That doesn't make the tender closed, and firms do unseat incumbents, especially where the current supplier has fallen out of favour.

Find out what you can. Check whether the work has been tendered before and ask in a clarification question whether there is an incumbent. If the buyer ran preliminary market engagement, the Act says it must make sure no supplier gets an unfair advantage from it, and must exclude a supplier whose advantage can't be avoided ([Procurement Act 2023, section 16](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/54/section/16)).

### 4. Do you have evidence from comparable work?

Quality questions are usually scored on evidence: case studies, references and results from similar contracts. Buyers can't demand that you have worked for one particular authority before, but they can ask for evidence of comparable delivery. Use relevant private sector work if you have it. If you have nothing comparable to show, the bid will be hard to score well.

### 5. Does the contract fit your size?

Compare the value band with your turnover, team and insurance. A contract that would double your business overnight is a risk for you and the buyer, and evaluators can see it. Look for lots, which are smaller parts of a larger contract, or consider a subcontracting role or a consortium with a partner whose capacity complements yours.

### 6. Do you have time to do it properly?

Check the closing date against your diary. A strong bid needs time to read the documents, ask clarification questions, gather evidence, price and review. Finding a tender a few days before it closes is one of the most common reasons small firms submit weak bids or don't bid at all.

### 7. Is the prize worth the cost of bidding?

Estimate the hours the bid will take across everyone involved, then cost them at your normal rate. Compare that with the margin you'd earn if you won, scaled by an honest view of your chances. If the cost of bidding is close to the profit you'd make, the tender only makes sense when it opens a door, such as a first reference in a new sector.

## A simple bid no bid matrix

Score each line 0, 1 or 2 and be strict with yourself. Any 0 on the first line is a no bid on its own.

| Question | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pass/fail requirements | We fail one | We can meet them with work | We meet all of them now |
| Price weighting | Mostly price and we're not cheapest | Balanced | Mostly quality |
| Incumbent | Strong incumbent bidding | Unknown | No incumbent, or clearly unhappy buyer |
| Comparable evidence | None | Some, from other sectors | Strong, recent and similar |
| Fit with our size | Far too big or small | Stretch, or lot available | Comfortable fit |
| Time to deadline | Not enough | Tight | Enough to do it well |
| Value of winning | Low margin, no strategic value | Fair margin | Good margin or strategic door-opener |

Agree your cut-off before you start, for example bidding only where the total clears a level you've set and there are no zeros. The point isn't the exact score. It's making the decision on purpose, the same way every time, rather than chasing every notice that looks interesting.

## Signs a tender may already be leaning one way

None of these prove a contract is decided, but each one should lower your score on question 3.

- The specification is thin or unusually specific, such as describing one product or way of working.
- The deadline is very short for the size of the work.
- Clarification questions go unanswered or come back late.
- The requirements, such as references or team sizes, match one existing supplier closely.
- The buyer's earlier market engagement pointed clearly towards a particular approach that isn't yours.

When you see two or more, ask a direct clarification question. If the answer doesn't reassure you, it's usually a no bid.

## What to do when the answer is no

A no bid doesn't have to be a dead end.

- **Go smaller.** Lower-value contracts often run lighter processes and are a good way to build public sector references.
- **Subcontract.** Delivering part of a contract for a larger supplier gives you public sector evidence, and the 30-day payment terms implied into public contracts also apply to public sub-contracts ([Procurement Act 2023, section 73](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/54/section/73)).
- **Get on frameworks.** Under the Act, open frameworks reopen at set points so new suppliers can join ([GCA on open frameworks](https://www.gca.gov.uk/news/how-gca-is-improving-opportunities-for-smes-and-vcses-supplier-specifics)).
- **Watch for the re-tender.** Make a note of the contract and when it might come back, so you can prepare early next time.

## After a loss, read your assessment summary

For a competitively tendered public contract, the buyer must send an assessment summary to every supplier whose tender it assessed, before the award notice goes out ([Assessing competitive tenders guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/procurement-act-2023-guidance-documents-procure-phase/assessing-competitive-tenders-html)). Read it line by line. It tells you whether you lost on price, on a particular quality question or on evidence, and that feeds straight back into your next bid no bid decision. Bidders who have done this for years say to ask for a feedback call too, where the buyer can be more candid.

## FAQ

### What is a bid no bid decision?

It is a structured check you run before preparing a tender response, to decide whether the opportunity is worth the time and cost of bidding. It usually covers eligibility, price weighting, competition, evidence, fit with your size, time and the value of winning.

### Is tendering worth it for a small business?

It can be, when you bid selectively. Public sector work can be steady and well paid, but each bid takes real time. Small firms that do well tend to bid for fewer, better-matched tenders rather than everything they could technically deliver.

### Should I bid if there is an incumbent supplier?

Sometimes. An incumbent often has an advantage, but contracts do change hands, particularly where the buyer wants something different. Ask a clarification question, check for warning signs, and bid when you have a clear reason the buyer would switch.

### How do I know how a tender will be scored?

The buyer must publish the award criteria and their relative importance in the tender notice or documents. Read these before deciding to bid, because they tell you what the buyer values most.

### What should I do after losing a tender?

Read the assessment summary the buyer sends, ask for a feedback call if one is offered, and update your evidence and answers. Then use what you learned in your next bid no bid decision.
