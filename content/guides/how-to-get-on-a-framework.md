**Short answer:** a public sector framework is a contract that lets a buyer award later work, called call-offs, to the suppliers already on it. If you are not on the framework, you usually cannot bid for that work. ## What a framework is, and what it is not

The Act defines a framework as a contract between a contracting authority and one or more suppliers that provides for the future award of contracts to those suppliers ([section 45(2)](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/54/section/45)). In plain terms, the buyer (or a centralised procurement authority acting for many buyers) runs a competition once, appoints a list of suppliers, and then awards individual pieces of work from that list.

Two points are easy to miss.

First, a place on the framework is not a contract for work. Most frameworks do not commit the buyer to buy anything from you. People who have used them describe a place as a fishing licence: it lets you fish in that pond, it does not land the catch.

Second, a lot of public spending never appears as an open tender notice. If the work is being called off an existing framework, only the suppliers already on it will be invited. [How to find public sector and government tenders](/guides/how-to-find-public-sector-tenders) covers the notices you will see. This guide is about getting into the lists that those later contracts are drawn from.

The Act uses the word "framework" rather than "framework agreement", but buyers and suppliers still say framework agreement. The legal idea is the same.

## Why this matters if you are a small firm

Small firms often spend weeks on advertised tenders, then find that much of the work in their sector is only offered to suppliers already appointed to a framework. That is true in [technology](/categories/technology), [construction](/categories/construction-infrastructure) and [professional services](/categories/professional-services) alike. The consistent advice from people who buy, and from people who have won, is to get on the relevant lists, then compete for the call-offs.

A framework application is itself a tender, so it deserves a proper [bid or no bid decision](/guides/bid-no-bid-decision). The unpaid hours can be significant, and a place still does not guarantee work. The payoff is access: buyers named as users of the framework can award you call-offs without running a full new procurement from scratch. Miss the list, and you can be locked out for years.

## The three tools you will see

Under the Act you will meet three related tools. They are easy to confuse, and the names have changed.

### Standard frameworks

A standard framework is appointed once, usually after a competitive tender. New suppliers cannot join until it is replaced. The maximum term is generally four years, or eight years for a defence and security framework or a utilities framework, unless the buyer can justify a longer term in the tender or transparency notice ([section 47](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/54/section/47)). Call-off contracts can run beyond the end of the framework.

If you miss the application window, you wait. That is the closed-door problem people complain about.

### Open frameworks

An open framework is a scheme of successive frameworks awarded on substantially the same terms ([section 49](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/54/section/49)). The point is to let new suppliers in at intervals, rather than locking the list for the whole term.

Except where only one supplier is appointed, it must be reopened at least once in its first three years and at least every five years after that, and can last eight years in total. The buyer can reopen more often, including every year. If you miss the first appointment, watch for the reopen date rather than trying to talk your way onto a closed list.

### Dynamic markets

A dynamic market is not a contract and not a framework. It is a list of qualified suppliers who are eligible to compete for later contracts ([Dynamic Markets guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/procurement-act-2023-guidance-documents-define-phase/guidance-dynamic-markets-html)). It replaces the old dynamic purchasing system (DPS) and qualification system.

The features that matter for a small firm are that you can apply at any time, the buyer must consider applications in a reasonable period, the number of members cannot be capped, and the conditions for membership cannot be changed mid-way through. Contracts under the market must be awarded using the competitive flexible procedure. If a contract is advertised and you are not yet a member, you can usually apply to join and the buyer must consider that application before shutting you out, unless there is genuinely no time because of the complexity of that procurement.

For many small firms this is the more realistic first step, because you do not have to wait for a once-every-four-years window. These rules apply to new procurements under the Act in England, Wales and Northern Ireland. Devolved Scottish authorities mostly buy under Scotland's own procurement law.

## How you get a place

You get onto a standard or open framework by winning the competition (or, in limited cases, through a direct award of the framework itself, which cannot be used for an open framework). Treat it like any other tender. Read the conditions of participation — insurance, financial standing, accreditations and policies. For a public contract, a buyer cannot require insurance to be in place before award, but you must show you will have it ([section 22](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/54/section/22)). Answer quality questions with evidence, not promises; [how to bid for and win council contracts](/guides/how-to-win-council-contracts) covers how that scoring works. Check who may use the framework, what lots it is split into, and whether supplier numbers are capped. Prepare case studies of comparable work. They do not have to be public sector if the question asks for similar delivery, but they do have to be relevant.

You get onto a dynamic market by meeting its conditions for membership. Those conditions must be a proportionate test of legal and financial capacity or technical ability. The buyer cannot insist on audited accounts if you are not required to have them audited, cannot insist that insurance is already in place before a later contract is awarded, and cannot require you to have worked for one particular authority.

Have the pack ready before you apply: policies, insurance details, accreditations, and a few reusable case studies. Many [technology](/categories/technology) opportunities ask for Cyber Essentials or an equivalent, and certification takes time, so start early. Central digital and cloud frameworks still work like other frameworks: you apply during an opening, you are appointed to lots, and buyers then call off. A place does not mean every public body will buy from you.

## Call-off contracts: you still have to win the work

Once you are on a framework, work is awarded as call-off contracts. The selection process must be set out in the framework itself ([section 45](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/54/section/45)).

There are two main routes. The first is a competitive selection process, often called a mini-competition, between the suppliers on the relevant lot. The buyer can only assess proposals against some or all of the original award criteria, though those criteria can be refined. The second is an award without further competition, but only if there is more than one supplier and the framework sets out both an objective mechanism for choosing the supplier — for example highest ranking, or a rotation — and the core terms of the call-off. If those rules are not in the framework, the buyer should not direct-award from it.

Buyers must publish a contract award notice when they award a call-off, with limited exceptions. On a dynamic market, each contract is a competitive flexible procedure among members. Membership only gets you into the competition; you still have to bid.

If the first time a buyer hears of you is when a call-off lands, you are often behind suppliers who used the months before to understand the need. ## Fees and terms you should check

Fees are regulated, and this surprises people who have been asked to pay to "access" a list.

For a framework, fees may only be charged to suppliers who are awarded a call-off, and only as a fixed percentage of the estimated value of that call-off. The percentage cannot change during the life of the framework. It is not permissible to charge you to gain access, or to charge other management fees for being on the list ([section 45(7)](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/54/section/45)). For a dynamic market that is not a utilities dynamic market, fees can only be charged to suppliers awarded a contract under the market, as a fixed percentage of that contract's estimated value ([section 38](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/54/section/38)).

Also check the estimated value of the framework and of each lot, which authorities are entitled to use it, the reopen dates if it is an open framework, and whether you would be a single supplier (a single-supplier open framework has a shorter maximum term). If a fee or access charge does not match those rules, treat that as a warning sign and take advice before you apply.

## A practical path for a small firm

You do not need to be on every framework in your sector. You need to be on the ones your buyers actually use, in the lots you can deliver. List the categories and regions you can evidence, such as [construction](/categories/construction-infrastructure), [technology](/categories/technology) or [professional services](/categories/professional-services). Watch for new framework competitions and for dynamic market intention notices — dynamic markets are often the fastest legal way in, because they stay open. Qualify each opening as you would a tender, apply with a reusable pack of case studies, policies, insurance and accreditations, and once appointed watch for call-offs. A place you never bid from is worth little. If you cannot get a place yet, subcontracting to a supplier who is already on the list, or bidding for advertised work that is not a call-off, can give you the evidence for the next opening.

## Common mistakes

Do not treat appointment as the win. Do not assume a closed window is the end of the road — look for an open-framework reopen or a dynamic market in the same space. Avoid applying for lots you cannot evidence, or joining a list your target buyers are not entitled to use. Do not pay a joining fee: access should not be charged that way. And do not start a call-off bid without reading the selection process in the framework; a mini-competition is not the same as a fresh open tender.

## FAQs

### Does getting on a framework guarantee me work?

No. It only lets eligible buyers award you call-offs, which you still have to win.

### What is the difference between a framework and a dynamic market?

A framework is a contract with appointed suppliers, and a standard one may stay closed until it ends. A dynamic market is a list of qualified suppliers that must stay open, and contracts under it are awarded through a competitive flexible procedure.

### Can I join after it has started?

Not a standard closed framework, unless you wait for its replacement. An open framework must reopen on a set cycle. A dynamic market must accept applications at any time.

### Do I have to pay to get on?

The buyer may not charge you to gain access. Fees are only allowed, where they are set out in the documents, as a fixed percentage of a call-off or of a contract awarded under a dynamic market.

### Are council frameworks different?

The same Act rules apply to new procurements in England, Wales and Northern Ireland. Start with the lists your target buyers actually use, including [council tenders](/guides/council-tenders-uk) and [IT tenders](/guides/it-tenders-uk).
